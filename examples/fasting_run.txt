  The research set out to determine whether intermittent fasting delivers measurable metabolic benefits (insulin sensitivity, HbA1c, lipids, inflammation) beyond what an equivalent calorie deficit from conventional dieting would produce, and whether the evidence is strong enough to justify IF-specific guidance in a corporate wellness program. The source set includes several peer-reviewed RCTs and systematic reviews that are individually credible, but collectively they fall short of providing a confident answer. The strongest isocaloric RCT (s1, Lin et al. 2024 in Annals of Internal Medicine) found no TRE-specific benefit when calories were held equal, while a secondary analysis of an ADF trial (s5, Varady et al. 2019) showed a large HOMA-IR improvement favoring ADF — but that study is older, is a post-hoc subgroup analysis with imperfect weight-loss matching, and the effect may partly reflect greater weight loss in the ADF arm rather than a true fasting mechanism. Two meta-analyses (s6, s9) report modest short-term insulin improvements with fasting strategies, but neither clearly separates isocaloric from ad libitum designs, meaning calorie-deficit confounding cannot be ruled out. Two sources (s2, s8) were completely inaccessible (403 errors), and the ScienceDaily summary of ChronoFast (s3) describes a trial comparing early vs. late TRE — not TRE vs. unrestricted eating — so using it as evidence against IF broadly is a design mismatch. No source in the set provides the actual peer-reviewed ChronoFast paper or the Chen et al. 2024 meta-analysis named in the intent. Overall, the evidence assembled here is insufficient to make a confident, actionable recommendation to a VP: the isocaloric evidence is thin and conflicting, key recent studies are missing or unreachable, and the distinction between calorie-driven and fasting-specific benefits remains unresolved. This research should not be relied upon in its current form without filling the gaps noted below.
